What is Adhik Maas?

The Hindu calendar's elegant solution to a cosmic mismatch

The Hindu calendar is lunisolar — it tracks both the Moon and the Sun. Every few years, a gap builds up between them. Adhik Maas is how the tradition bridges that gap — and in doing so, creates the most spiritually potent month of the cycle.

The solar year is approximately 365.25 days. The lunar year — 12 lunar months — is approximately 354 days. That leaves a gap of roughly 11 days per year. Over 32–33 months, this gap accumulates to a full lunar month. Rather than letting the calendar drift out of sync, the Hindu tradition inserts an extra (adhik) month to realign. Adhik means additional, extra. The month it falls in this year is Jyeshtha — so it is called Adhik Jyeshtha Maas. The festivals that fall in that month are postponed to the regular Jyeshtha that follows — which is why Diwali and Raksha Bandhan shift later in years with an Adhik Maas.

The Astronomy — Why It Happens

In the Hindu lunar calendar, each month begins with a new moon and ends with the next new moon — approximately 29.5 days. The solar calendar, which governs seasons and agricultural cycles, is based on the Earth's revolution around the Sun — approximately 365.25 days.


The key signal for Adhik Maas: each regular lunar month contains one Sankranti — the Sun crossing from one zodiac sign to another (12 signs × 1 crossing each = 12 Sankrantis per year, one per regular month). But occasionally a lunar month passes with no Sankranti at all — the Sun doesn't change signs during that entire moonth. That month is declared Adhik — extra. It falls outside the normal system and is given to Lord Vishnu directly, making it his personal month.


This astronomical event occurs approximately every 32–33 months — roughly once every 2.7 years. The last Adhik Maas before 2026 was in 2023 (Adhik Shravan). The next after 2026 will be in 2028.

From Mal Maas to Purushottam Maas

How an unwanted month became the most blessed one

1

The Month Nobody Wanted

In ancient times, the extra month was called Mal Maas — the dirty or impure month. Since it had no ruling solar deity (no Sankranti), it was considered spiritually invalid for auspicious activities. No weddings, no Griha Pravesh, no major religious ceremonies. The presiding deity of each regular month is a sun-aspect who gives the month its character and blessings. This extra month had no such deity — it was, in the language of the Puranas, unclaimed. The representatives of the month came to the assembly of the gods and lamented: "We have no name, no lord, no identity. We are the homeless month."
2

The Month Goes to Lord Vishnu

According to the Padma Purana, the presiding spirit of the extra month — dejected and nameless — travelled to Vaikuntha and prostrated before Lord Vishnu. "Every month has a ruling deity," the month said, "but I have none. I am rejected. No one worships in me. I am called Mal Maas — the unclean month." Lord Vishnu, moved by this humility, declared: "From this day, this month belongs to me. I myself will be its lord. It will be called Purushottam Maas — the month of Purushottama, the Highest Being." He then made a promise recorded in the Padma Purana: any spiritual act performed during this month — any prayer, any charity, any fast, any reading of scripture — will yield Akshaya Phala, everlasting merit, multiplied many times over. The month that was once rejected became the most spiritually potent of all.
3

King Nahush — The Power of Mal Maas Vrat

The Padma Purana records the story of King Nahush, who found himself bound by severe karmic debts and was unable to attain the throne of Indra despite his great merit in other respects. On the advice of Sage Agastya, he observed the Purushottam Maas Vrat — fasting, worship and charity throughout the extra month. Through the power of this observance, his karmic bondages were dissolved and he attained the throne of Indra. This story illustrates the tradition's understanding of Adhik Maas: it is specifically designed for clearing karmic debts and accelerating spiritual progress — the very things that regular religious activities cannot always accomplish.

What to Do During Adhik Maas

This is a month of turning inward — not of external achievement

The Padma Purana states: even lighting a single lamp during Adhik Maas carries more merit than an entire year of the same act in a regular month. This is the month where the smallest sincere act is received by Vishnu himself — because he has personally claimed this month as his own.

📖

Read Sacred Texts Daily

The Bhagavata Purana, Vishnu Purana, Purushottam Maas Katha, Bhagavad Gita, and Vishnu Sahasranama are all traditionally recited during this month. Even one chapter or one page daily, read with intention, carries enormous merit. The month asks you not to rush — it asks you to slow down and absorb. If you have been meaning to read the Gita for years, this is the month to finally begin.
🙏

Vishnu Puja and Deepdan

Daily worship of Lord Vishnu is the heart of Adhik Maas. Offer Tulsi leaves — Vishnu's most beloved — along with flowers, incense, and a lamp. Deepdan (lamp offering) to Vishnu every evening is considered especially powerful. Chant the Vishnu Sahasranama or simply Om Namo Bhagavate Vasudevaya. Satyanarayan Puja performed during this month carries the merit of a year's worth of regular observance.
🤲

Anna Daan — Feed the Hungry

Charity and feeding the poor during Adhik Maas is given extraordinary emphasis in all Puranic sources. Anna Daan (food donation), Vastra Daan (clothing), and Til Daan (sesame seeds) are all mentioned specifically. The merit of charity in this month is said to be multiplied tenfold. Whatever you can give — give more generously this month than usual. This is the single act the texts emphasise above all others.
🧘

Fast on Ekadashi — Especially Parama Ekadashi

Adhik Maas contains two Ekadashis — Parama Ekadashi (27 May) and Padmini Ekadashi (11 June). Parama Ekadashi is considered the most powerful Ekadashi in the entire Hindu calendar — fasting on this single day is said to carry the merit of all 24 Ekadashis of the year. Even if you observe no other fasts, observe this one. Break the fast on Dwadashi (the following day) with Tulsi water and simple food.
🌅

Brahma Muhurta — Wake Before Sunrise

The pre-dawn hours (approximately 4:00–5:30 AM) are called Brahma Muhurta — the Creator's Hour, when the mind is naturally still and receptive. Rising during Brahma Muhurta for even part of Adhik Maas and spending those minutes in silence, prayer or reading is considered an act of exceptional spiritual merit. The month is 30 days — even 10 of those mornings practiced consistently will transform your inner life.
🛐

Satsang and Katha

Listening to or participating in Bhagavat Katha (recitation of the Bhagavata Purana stories) or Purushottam Maas Katha during this month is considered especially meritorious. Many temples conduct extended sessions of discourse and katha throughout the month. Even listening to recordings of scripture or watching a katha online counts — the key is the intention of receiving the knowledge.

What to Avoid — and Why

Not superstition — a logical consequence of the month's nature

Worldly Auspicious Activities — Postpone, Not Cancel

Because Adhik Maas has no Sankranti and no ruling solar deity, major worldly milestone events are traditionally avoided — not because the month is inauspicious for spiritual life, but because it operates in a different mode:

  • Weddings and engagements
  • Griha Pravesh (housewarming ceremonies)
  • Mundan (first haircut ceremony)
  • Upanayana (sacred thread ceremony)
  • Major new business launches with ceremonial opening

Note: These are traditional guidelines. Modern life does not always allow postponement, and sincere worship during Adhik Maas is never wrong. The intent behind the guidelines is to use this month for spiritual deepening rather than worldly milestones — not to create anxiety.

Key Mantras for Adhik Maas

Vishnu is the lord of this month — these are his direct invocations

The Primary Vishnu Mantra

ॐ नमो भगवते वासुदेवाय
Om Namo Bhagavate Vasudevaya
"Salutations to Lord Vasudeva (Vishnu/Krishna)." The twelve-syllable Dwadashakshari mantra — the most complete Vishnu invocation. Chant 108 times daily during Adhik Maas for maximum benefit.

How Adhik Maas Shifts Festival Dates in 2026

Why Diwali and Raksha Bandhan are later this year

The Cascade Effect

Because Adhik Maas inserts an extra month between May and June, all festivals that follow it in the Hindu lunar year shift approximately 30 days later in the Gregorian calendar than they would in a non-Adhik year. This is why:

  • Raksha Bandhan 2026 — falls in late August instead of early August
  • Janmashtami 2026 — falls in early September instead of August
  • Ganesh Chaturthi 2026 — mid-September
  • Navratri (Sharad) 2026 — October instead of late September
  • Diwali 2026 — 8 November instead of mid-October

This is entirely normal and expected — the Hindu calendar is self-correcting. The festivals are not delayed — they are falling exactly where they should in the lunar calendar. The Gregorian calendar is what appears shifted.

Adhik Maas for the Modern World

A month the universe built in for slowness — in a world that never stops

The Gift of an Extra Month

In a world governed by quarterly targets, annual reviews, and relentless productivity, Adhik Maas arrives with a counter-cultural invitation: slow down. This month has no Sankranti — no solar milestone, no celestial checkpoint. It is, by design, a month outside the normal flow of time. The tradition's response to this astronomical anomaly was not anxiety but joy — Lord Vishnu claimed it and filled it with spiritual potential. The modern invitation is the same: use this extra month not to achieve more, but to become more. Read what you have been meaning to read. Give more than you normally would. Sit in silence more than you usually do. The universe has handed you a bonus month — spend it wisely.

For Diaspora Families — A Month to Reconnect

For Hindu families living outside India, Adhik Maas offers a specific and practical invitation: use these 30 days to reconnect with the tradition more intentionally than usual. Choose one scripture to read together as a family. Set up a small Vishnu puja corner at home. Do one act of charity per week. Observe at least one Ekadashi fast — especially Parama Ekadashi on 27 May. The month does not require elaborate ceremonies or temple access. It requires sincerity and a turning of attention toward what is deeper. That is available anywhere in the world.

A Simple 30-Day Practice
  • Light a lamp before the Vishnu/Krishna image every evening
  • Read one page of the Bhagavad Gita or Bhagavata daily
  • Chant Om Namo Bhagavate Vasudevaya 108 times at least once
  • Fast on Parama Ekadashi (27 May) — the most powerful single day
  • Give to someone in need every week — food, money or time
  • Observe Purushottam Purnima (31 May) with special puja and charity
  • Wake 15 minutes earlier than usual — use that time in silence
