As evening settles over Varanasi, the steps down to the Ganga begin to sparkle. Families, pilgrims and volunteers set earthen lamps along the riverfront. One flame becomes a hundred; their reflections gather into a ribbon of gold. On Dev Deepavali, the city’s devotion becomes something you can see.

01 · Kartik Purnima · Varanasi

A different night of light

Dev Deepavali means the “Diwali of the Gods.” It belongs to Kartik Purnima, the full moon of Kartik, and is especially associated with Kashi, another name for Varanasi. Devotional belief welcomes the gods to the sacred river on this night. Official festival introduction

It follows the better-known autumn Diwali, usually by about a fortnight. Their names share the language of lamps, but they are distinct observances. Kartik’s sacred season also includes Tulsi Vivah; households and temples follow their own calendars and customs.

02 · Kartik Purnima · Varanasi

Shiva and the three cities

A sacred story from the Shiva Purana, Rudra-samhita, Yuddha-khanda.

The boon of three cities

Three brothers had won an extraordinary promise, but not the one they first sought. Tarakaksha, Vidyunmali and Kamalaksha, sons of Taraka, had performed severe austerities. When Brahma appeared, they asked to be free from death. He refused: they must choose another boon.

So they asked for three fortified cities. Maya built them in gold, silver and iron. Their destruction would require a rare conjunction: the cities must come together, and Shiva must pierce them with a single arrow. Within those walls, life flourished. There were gardens, lakes and temples, and the inhabitants worshipped Shiva. The boon and the cities

Why Shiva would not strike

When the devas, distressed by the power of Tripura, sought his help, Shiva did not immediately take up his weapon. The rulers were his devotees and still practised meritorious deeds. He would not knowingly harm them while that devotion endured. Their protection rested on more than their mighty walls. The devas’ appeal · Shiva’s response

The story then turns on divine illusion. Vishnu created a teacher who, with Narada’s help, persuaded the brothers and their people to leave their former worship. Once they had abandoned Shiva’s worship and their virtuous conduct, the devas returned to seek his intervention. The turning point · The renewed appeal

The moment of the single arrow

A divine chariot was prepared, and Brahma became its charioteer. Shiva mounted it and set out towards the cities. The Purana gives this a cosmic scale: even the Vedas become the horses that draw his chariot. Shiva’s campaign

Yet the decisive moment had not arrived. The three cities remained apart. After Shiva honoured Ganesha, they came together. Shiva drew his bow and released the blazing arrow; the three fortresses were reduced to ashes. He is remembered as Tripurantaka, the destroyer of Tripura, and Tripurari, its vanquisher. The fall of Tripura

On Tripurari Purnima, this victory is remembered through lamps and worship. The sacred story and Kashi’s living celebration meet in that offering of light. The festival connection

03 · Kartik Purnima · Varanasi

An offering of light

For some pilgrims, the day begins with Kartik snan, a sacred bath. Others honour the occasion through prayer or charity. Bathing is one customary observance, not something every visitor or household must do. Kartik Purnima observances

At dusk comes deepdaan, the offering of light. Earthen diyas shine along the steps while devotees gather for prayers and Ganga Aarti, the ceremonial offering of lamps to the river. The small lamps and the larger aarti flames have their own place in the evening’s worship. Celebrations in Varanasi

04 · Kartik Purnima · Varanasi

Kashi, seen from the river

From the water, the illuminated steps reveal the sweep of Kashi’s riverfront. From the ghats, the same lights stretch away beside the Ganga. Boats, bells and devotional music form part of the scene, but its heart is the shared act of offering. Kashi’s riverfront celebration

The illustrations here evoke that atmosphere. They are devotional artwork, not photographs of a particular year’s event or exact maps of the ghats.

05 · Kartik Purnima · Varanasi

A light to carry home

A single diya cannot light the whole riverbank. Together, many small flames change what everyone sees. We can carry that image home as a personal reflection: one patient conversation, one generous act, one moment of gratitude can bring light into an ordinary day.

May Dev Deepavali bring peace, clarity and goodwill to every home.
