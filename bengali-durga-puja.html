Bengali Durga Puja

A goddess’s homecoming, the courage of Shakti and the tenderness of Bijoya

16–21 Oct 2026বিজয়া · 21 OctApprox. 9-minute read

In Bengal, Maa Durga is not only the goddess who vanquishes Mahishasura. She is also Uma, the beloved daughter returning home each autumn. Her festival holds victory and farewell, art and devotion, family and community together.

Why Durga Puja is more than a festival

In Bengal, Durga Puja (দুর্গাপূজা) is at once a sacred celebration, an artistic collaboration and a beloved reunion. For a few days, neighbourhood streets become galleries of light and craft, the beat of the dhak announces the season, families return home, and the goddess is welcomed as an honoured daughter. Although worship of Durga has different forms across India, the Bengali autumn celebration has a distinct emotional heart: the joy of arrival and the tenderness of farewell.

The goddess who overcame Mahishasura

In the Devi Mahatmya, part of the Markandeya Purana, the gods face the asura Mahishasura, whose strength and shape-shifting power have brought disorder. Their combined divine radiance manifests as the goddess Durga, and they present her with weapons. After battle, she defeats Mahishasura. This is the spiritual centre of Mahishasuramardini—the victory of Shakti, moral courage and clarity over domination and arrogance. Bengali idols often portray this moment: a multi-armed Durga, her lion, and Mahishasura. This is a sacred narrative and its lessons are matters of faith and interpretation, not a claim of verifiable historical events.

Why Bengal calls her Uma, the daughter coming home

Alongside the cosmic warrior story is a deeply affectionate regional telling. Durga is Uma or Parvati, daughter of the Himalaya; the autumn festival is imagined as her annual visit to her parents’ home, bringing her children. The familiar Bengali idol grouping includes Lakshmi, Saraswati, Ganesha and Kartikeya. In this cherished domestic imagination, Dashami is not simply the end of a ritual. It is the moment when a daughter leaves for her husband Shiva’s home on Kailash. The joyful anticipation of “Maa eshechhen” (Mother has arrived) becomes the bittersweet refrain “Aschhe bochhor abar hobe” (We will celebrate again next year). This homecoming is a Bengali devotional and cultural interpretation; it is not the same storyline as the Devi Mahatmya’s battle.

From family worship to shared neighbourhood art

Bengal’s puja has long included household traditions, while sarbojanin or community pujas made collective worship, financing and participation central to urban life. Artisans—particularly clay sculptors associated with Kumartuli—shape the idols, while designers, carpenters, lighting artists, musicians, volunteers and local businesses bring pandals to life. In 2021 UNESCO inscribed “Durga Puja in Kolkata” on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognising its living blend of worship, art and community. There is no single correct pandal design; some follow a classical ek-chala arrangement, while others create ambitious contemporary installations.

How a Bengali Durga Puja comes alive: pandals and idol-making

From bamboo frame to clay goddess. In Bengal, the idol is often created through a layered craft tradition refined over generations, especially in Kolkata’s Kumartuli. A basic framework is made with bamboo or cane and tied with straw to shape the body. Artisans then apply layers of clay—first a rough layer to form volume, then a smoother finishing layer for the face, hands and details. After drying, the image is polished, painted and dressed. The eyes are especially significant; the final drawing of the gaze, often called chokkhu daan, is treated with great reverence.

Adornment and iconography. Many idols are decorated with fabric, jewellery, crowns, weapons and decorative materials such as shola. Some traditional images use the ornate daaker saaj style, known for its shimmering silver-toned embellishment. The familiar family grouping places Durga at the centre with Lakshmi, Saraswati, Ganesha and Kartikeya, while the lion and Mahishasura complete the dramatic scene of victory.

What a pandal really is. A pandal is much more than a tent or temporary stage. It is a shared neighbourhood creation—part temple, part artwork, part gathering space. Some pandals recreate classical temple forms; others tell social, historical or ecological stories through elaborate themes. Designers, carpenters, electricians, lighting artists, painters, sculptors, priests, dhaakis, cooks and volunteers all contribute to the experience.

The life around the puja. Long queues, evening lights, dhak rhythms, bhog distribution and pandal-hopping are all part of the festival atmosphere. Many committees also think about crowd flow, safety and increasingly eco-conscious materials so that immersion is more respectful to rivers and lakes. Not every puja follows the same style, but the common spirit is clear: devotion expressed through craftsmanship, hospitality and community pride.

Important distinction: Bengal observes Bijoya Dashami on Wednesday, 21 October 2026, while many other parts of India observe Vijayadashami on Tuesday, 20 October. This difference is supported by the Government of India’s national calendar and the Kolkata Bengal-rule almanac. Verify detailed ritual timings with your local panjika.

The five main days, and what each one means

Mahalaya marks an important threshold with ancestral remembrance and invocation of the goddess; many Bengalis begin the season by listening to Mahishasuramardini, the beloved dawn broadcast. Shashthi welcomes the goddess, with bodhan (awakening/invocation), amantran and adhivas in many traditions. Saptami includes Nabapatrika worship: nine plants are bound together, bathed and installed; the banana plant is popularly called Kola Bou, but it is inaccurate to treat the whole ritual simply as Ganesha’s wedding. Ashtami features pushpanjali (flower offerings), worship and, at the Ashtami–Navami junction, Sandhi Puja commemorating the fierce aspect of the goddess. Navami continues worship, homa in many traditions, bhog and community gatherings. Dashami brings farewell rites and immersion. Timings depend on local panjika and individual puja committee practice.

Dhak, dhunuchi, alpona and the art of gathering

The deep, rolling sound of the dhak is inseparable from the Bengali puja atmosphere. Dhunuchi naach uses an incense-filled earthen vessel in a celebratory dance, especially during evening aarti. Alpona, usually painted in white rice paste, decorates floors with lotus forms, footsteps and intricate patterns. Visitors practise pandal-hopping, meet old friends, watch cultural performances and share meals. Puja is also a major livelihood season for artisans, cooks, weavers, transport workers and countless neighbourhood businesses. Some of its most meaningful features are social: volunteering, craftsmanship and making room for many kinds of participants.

What people eat and share

Community bhog frequently centres on khichuri, labra (mixed vegetables), chutney and payesh, though menus vary by puja and household. Across Bengal, street food, festive restaurant meals and homemade dishes are important parts of the celebration. After Dashami, Bijoya visits often come with narkel naru, sandesh, rosogolla, nimki and other sweets and savouries. These are examples, not a prescribed menu; regional, religious and household preferences differ.

What happens on Bijoya Dashami

The farewell can begin with Devi Baran or Boron: in many communities married women honour the goddess with sweets, betel leaves and sindoor. Sindoor Khela, traditionally associated with married Hindu women, follows in many pujas; participation and inclusivity now vary among communities. The image is then taken for visarjan (immersion), often after symbolic farewell rites. Where immersion has ecological impacts, organisers can prefer approved immersion sites and environmentally considerate materials. The day’s mixed emotion is captured by “আসছে বছর আবার হবে” — “It will happen again next year.” Regional rites and their order are not uniform.

The deeper story behind “Shubho Bijoya”

“শুভ বিজয়া” (Shubho Bijoya) literally conveys an auspicious or blessed victory. The victory recalls Durga’s overcoming of Mahishasura, but in Bengali social life Bijoya is also a season of restored relationships. Younger people offer pronam to elders and receive blessings; relatives and friends exchange kolakuli (an embrace), and households welcome visitors with mishti. Visits, calls and messages may continue for days or weeks after the immersion. More than a generic “happy festival” phrase, it acknowledges both grief at a loved one’s departure and hope that she—and the bonds between people—will return stronger.

How to celebrate respectfully, even far from Bengal

Visit a community puja if you can, learn the story before arriving, and be considerate during aarti and worship. Ask before photographing people at prayer; respect queues and accessibility needs. If you are abroad, share a meal, call parents or grandparents, exchange greetings in Bengali, and support a local puja association. For children, explain both traditions—the cosmic victory and Uma’s visit home—without presenting them as competing facts. You do not need an elaborate idol or ritual to participate in the values of gratitude, courage, reunion and hospitality.

Shubho Bijoya — a message to share

শুভ বিজয়া! মায়ের আশীর্বাদে সকলের জীবন ভরে উঠুক শান্তি, সাহস, সুস্বাস্থ্য আর ভালোবাসায়।

Meaning: Shubho Bijoya! May Maa’s blessings fill everyone’s lives with peace, courage, good health and love.
