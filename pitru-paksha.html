📅 A Note on Dates

Pitru Paksha 2026 runs from Saturday 26 September to Saturday 10 October, beginning with Purnima Shraddha and ending on Sarva Pitru Amavasya. Because tithi-to-date mapping shifts with sunrise and location, dates can move by a day outside India — particularly in the Americas and Europe. Confirm your family's specific shraddh tithi with a current Panchang for your city, or with your purohit, before performing the rites.

What is Pitru Paksha?

The only major observance in the Hindu year with no deity at its centre

Every other observance in this series is addressed to a god. This one is addressed to your own family — to the people who raised the people who raised you, and who are no longer here to ask for anything.

Pitru Paksha — also called Shraddh Paksha or Mahalaya Paksha — is a sixteen-day period in the waning fortnight of Bhadrapada when Hindu families offer water, food and remembrance to their pitrs: the departed of three preceding generations. There is no idol, no aarti, no celebration. During these days no weddings are fixed, no houses are entered, no new ventures begun. The fortnight is reserved. It is not a fearful time or a mournful one — it is simply a debt being acknowledged, once a year, by a family that remembers where it came from.

Why Sixteen Days? The Story of Karna

The origin of Pitru Paksha, from the Mahabharata

1

The Most Generous Man Who Ever Lived

Karna was legendary for his generosity — he never refused anyone who came to him asking, giving away gold, weapons, even his own divine armour when asked. When he died on the battlefield of Kurukshetra, he ascended to the afterlife expecting the rewards of a lifetime of extraordinary charity.
2

Gold to Eat, and Nothing Else

In the afterlife, Karna was offered endless gold and jewels — but no food, no water. Starving and confused, he asked why. The answer was this: throughout his life Karna had donated wealth in abundance, but he had never made offerings of food and water to his own ancestors — because, raised by a charioteer's family and never knowing his true lineage until the very end, he had no ancestors he knew to honour.
3

Sixteen Days to Set It Right

Recognising that the omission was not Karna's fault, Yama granted him sixteen days to return to earth and perform the shraddh rites he had never known to do — offering food and water in his ancestors' memory. That sixteen-day window is Pitru Paksha. It is why the fortnight exists, and why its central offering is not gold or flowers but the two simplest things a human being needs: food and water.

Pitru Rin — The Debt You Were Born With

One of three debts Hindu tradition says every person carries

The Three Debts

Vedic tradition holds that every person is born owing three debts: Deva Rin (to the gods, repaid through worship), Rishi Rin (to the sages and teachers, repaid through study and passing on knowledge), and Pitru Rin — the debt to one's ancestors, repaid by raising a family well and by remembering those who came before. Pitru Paksha is the formal annual occasion for settling that third debt. It is not superstition or fear of ghosts; it is the tradition's way of building an obligation of memory into the calendar itself, so that no family can simply forget.

How to Find Your Ancestor's Shraddh Day

The question most guides skip entirely

Shraddh is performed on the tithi — the lunar day — on which your ancestor passed away, not on the Gregorian calendar date.

If your father died on the fifth day of the waning fortnight, his shraddh falls on Panchami during Pitru Paksha, regardless of what the English date was. If you know the Gregorian date of death, a Panchang or a purohit can convert it to the corresponding tithi for you. Some special cases follow their own rule: those who died a violent or accidental death are remembered on Chaturdashi; married women who predeceased their husbands are often remembered on Navami (Avidhava Navami); sanyasis on Dwadashi.

If You Do Not Know the Tithi

This is extremely common, especially for families living abroad or those whose records were lost across generations and migrations. The tradition anticipated it. Sarva Pitru Amavasya — 10 October 2026 — exists precisely for this. On that single day, shraddh may be offered for all ancestors at once, known and unknown, named and unnamed. If you remember nothing else from this guide, remember this one date. It is enough.

Tarpan at Home — Without a Priest

What the ritual actually requires is very little

1
Bathe and Prepare in the Morning

Perform tarpan after bathing, ideally during the Aparahna period (roughly midday to mid-afternoon), which is the traditional window for ancestral offerings. Wear clean, simple clothes.

2
Face South

Ancestors are invoked facing south — the direction associated with Yama and the realm of the departed. Sit on a mat or clean floor with a vessel of water before you.

3
Wear the Sacred Thread Over the Right Shoulder

Those who wear a janeu turn it over the right shoulder (apasavya) for ancestral rites — the reverse of its normal position. If you do not wear one, this step simply does not apply.

4
Offer Water with Black Sesame

Mix black sesame seeds (til) into the water. Pour it slowly from your cupped hands, letting it fall through the space between thumb and index finger — the pitru tirtha. Kusha grass, if available, is held in the hand while offering.

5
Say Their Names

Speak the names of those you are remembering — father, grandfather, great-grandfather; mother, grandmother, great-grandmother; and anyone else in the family you wish to include. This is the heart of the whole ritual. If you know no names, offer to "my ancestors" — it is accepted.

6
Offer Food — and Then Feed Someone

Prepare a simple satvik meal, traditionally including kheer, rice and vegetables. A portion is set aside for the ancestors, and the rest is shared — with a crow if possible, a cow, a dog, and above all with a person in need. The offering is not complete until someone living has eaten.

What is Set Aside

Why the calendar goes quiet for sixteen days

Customarily Deferred Until After Amavasya

These are traditions of restraint, not prohibitions born of fear. The fortnight is simply reserved for a different purpose:

  • Weddings, engagements and griha pravesh
  • Buying gold, vehicles, property or other auspicious purchases
  • Launching new businesses or major ventures
  • Non-vegetarian food, onion, garlic, alcohol and tobacco
  • Cutting hair or nails, on the shraddh day itself in many families

Note: modern life does not always allow deferral, and necessity is understood. The restraint lifts entirely the moment Sarva Pitru Amavasya ends — Sharad Navratri begins the very next morning.

Gaya — Where Pind Daan is Offered

The most important place in India for ancestral rites

Gaya in Bihar is considered the foremost site for Pind Daan — the offering of rice balls to ancestors. Tradition holds that rites performed at Gaya liberate ancestors permanently, ending the need for annual repetition.

During Pitru Paksha, the Gaya Ji Pitru Paksha Mela draws pilgrims from across the world to the Vishnupad Temple and the ghats of the Falgu river. Other significant sites include Haridwar on the Ganga, Varanasi, Nashik on the Godavari, and Rameswaram in the south. But the tradition is clear on this point: a pilgrimage is meritorious, not mandatory. Tarpan offered sincerely at home, with water and sesame and the names of the departed, is complete in itself.

ॐ पितामहेभ्यः स्वधायिभ्यः स्वधा नमः॥
Om Pitribhyah Svadhayibhyah Svadha Namah
Om Pitamahebhyah Svadhayibhyah Svadha Namah
"Salutations and offerings to my fathers, to my grandfathers, who receive with svadha." Svadha is the sacred word used specifically for offerings to ancestors, just as svaha is used for offerings to the gods.

Pitru Paksha for the Modern World

A culture that built remembrance into its calendar

Grief With a Place to Go

Most modern cultures have no structured, recurring occasion for remembering the dead beyond the first year. Grief is expected to resolve privately and then be set aside. Pitru Paksha takes the opposite approach: it gives grief a fixed annual address. Once a year, for sixteen days, a family is expected to speak the names of its dead out loud, prepare their food, and acknowledge that it exists because they did. For many people this turns out to be less a religious duty than a genuine psychological relief — permission, in a structured form, to remember.

For Diaspora Families — This One Travels

Pitru Paksha may be the easiest of all observances to keep far from India, because it asks for almost nothing that must be bought or found.

  • Water, black sesame and the names of your ancestors are enough — this is the complete ritual
  • If you don't know any tithi, use Sarva Pitru Amavasya on 10 October for everyone at once
  • Tell children who their great-grandparents were, and where the family came from — this is the actual content of the fortnight, not the ritual mechanics
  • Feeding someone in need is universally available and considered the highest offering of all
  • If you cannot do the full rite, a lamp, a moment of silence, and the names spoken aloud is a real observance — the tradition recognises sincerity over scale
