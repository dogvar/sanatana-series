# Tulsi Vivah — The Sacred Wedding
Sanatana Kathavachan | Based on the Devi Bhagavata Purana, Book Nine

In many Indian homes, there is a small Tulsi plant.

Every morning, someone may offer water, light a lamp or pause beside it for a moment of prayer.

But during the sacred month of Kartik, something extraordinary happens.

The Tulsi plant is adorned like a bride.

Fresh flowers surround it. Sugarcane stalks form a wedding canopy. Lamps are lit, families gather, and traditional wedding songs fill the home.

But why is a sacred plant married to Lord Vishnu?

To understand this beautiful tradition, we must travel into an ancient Puranic story.

The story of Tulasi and Shankhachuda.

Long ago, according to the sacred narrative, there lived a powerful ruler named Shankhachuda.

His wife, Tulasi, was renowned for her devotion and steadfastness.

Shankhachuda possessed divine armour that protected him in battle. His protection was also connected to the integrity of his marriage to Tulasi.

Then came a great conflict between Shankhachuda and the gods.

Even Lord Shiva entered the battle.

Yet Shankhachuda remained protected, and the struggle continued.

Lord Vishnu eventually approached him disguised as an elderly Brahmin and obtained his protective armour.

Then Vishnu assumed Shankhachuda's appearance and came before Tulasi.

Believing her husband had returned, Tulasi received him.

But the deception did not remain hidden.

When Tulasi discovered the truth, she was overcome with grief and anger.

She condemned Vishnu for what he had done and pronounced a curse: he would become stone.

According to this Puranic account, Vishnu accepted the curse.

The story then describes his association with the sacred Shaligram stone, while Tulasi becomes associated with the Gandaki River and the revered Tulsi plant.

And so, in this devotional tradition, Tulsi and Shaligram came to hold a special place together.

That ancient association is remembered every year through Tulsi Vivah.

The Tulsi plant is dressed and decorated as a bride. Shaligram, or another representation of Lord Vishnu, is honoured as the groom.

Families gather around the decorated plant, perform customary wedding rituals, light lamps, sing auspicious songs and share prasad.

The customs may differ between regions and households, but the celebration continues to connect devotion, nature and family traditions.

Wishing you and your family a blessed Tulsi Vivah.

This is an accessible literary retelling based on the Devi Bhagavata Purana. Other Puranic and regional traditions preserve different versions of Tulsi's sacred story.
