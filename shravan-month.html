📅 A Note on Dates — Your Region May Differ

Shravan's dates depend on which lunar calendar your family follows. In North India (UP, Bihar, Rajasthan, MP, Delhi, Punjab — Purnimanta calendar), Shravan runs 30 July – 28 August 2026. In Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana (Amanta calendar), Shravan runs 13 August – 11 September 2026. Both traditions honour the same essential month — only the calendar reckoning differs. Always confirm with your family's local Panchang.

Why an Entire Month for Shiva?

No other deity in the Hindu calendar receives this — a full month, undivided

Most Hindu festivals are single days or a handful of days. Shravan is different — the entire fifth month of the lunar year, all thirty days of it, is set aside for one deity alone.

Shravan, called Sawan in Hindi, falls during the monsoon — when the parched earth turns green, rivers swell, and the air itself feels charged. According to the Shiva Purana and Skanda Purana, worship performed during this month yields manifold results compared to any other time of year. Shiva is believed to be especially receptive to devotion during Shravan — sincere prayers are said to be answered more swiftly. To understand why this specific month became his, we have to go back to one of the most dramatic events in the entire Puranic cosmos: the churning of the ocean.

Samudra Manthan — Why Shravan Belongs to Shiva

The churning of the ocean, the deadliest poison, and the god who swallowed it

1

The Churning Begins

Long ago, the Devas (gods) and Asuras (demons) joined forces in an enormous cosmic undertaking: churning the great Ocean of Milk to obtain Amrit, the nectar of immortality. Using Mount Mandara as the churning rod and the serpent Vasuki as the rope, both sides pulled in opposite directions, turning the mountain against the ocean floor for what tradition says was thousands of years. As the churning progressed, the ocean began yielding extraordinary treasures — fourteen divine jewels emerged one by one, including Lakshmi, the celestial elephant Airavata, the wish-fulfilling cow Kamadhenu, and eventually Amrit itself.
2

Halahala — The Poison No One Wanted

Before the nectar emerged, the ocean released something else entirely: Halahala, a poison so deadly it threatened to annihilate the entire universe — gods, demons, and all of creation alike. As the toxic fumes spread, panic swept through both Devas and Asuras. No being in existence, however powerful, was willing or able to contain something so destructive. In desperation, they turned to the one deity capable of absorbing what nothing else could survive: Lord Shiva.
3

Neelkanth — The Blue-Throated One

Without hesitation, Shiva drank the Halahala to save all of creation. But rather than swallowing it fully — which would have destroyed even him — he held the poison in his throat, refusing to let it pass further into his body or spit it back out to harm the world. The poison's intensity turned his throat a deep blue, and from that moment on he was known as Neelkanth — the blue-throated one. The heat generated by the poison was so immense that the gods, alarmed, began pouring the sacred waters of the Ganga over him continuously to cool his body. Some traditions add that Indra, seeing Shiva's distress, caused the heavens themselves to open — bringing the monsoon rains that soothed him. This is why Shravan, the month of rain, became inseparably linked to Shiva: the very rains that fall during this month echo the waters once poured to cool the god who saved the universe.
4

Why Water Poured on Shiva Remains the Central Ritual

This is the theological root of Jalabhishek — the ritual of pouring water (and often milk) over the Shivling, performed with special devotion throughout Shravan and especially on the sacred Mondays. Every drop poured today is understood as a continuation of that same ancient act of cooling and honouring the god who took on the universe's poison so nothing else would have to. This is also why the Kanwar Yatra exists — thousands of devotees, dressed in saffron, travel on foot to fetch Ganga water from sacred sites and carry it, often for hundreds of kilometres, back to their local Shiva temples to perform this same abhishek during Shravan.

The Many Vrats of Shravan

Not just Mondays — the entire month layers observance upon observance

🌙

Shravan Somvar Vrat

The core observance — fasting every Monday of the month, performing Jalabhishek and Rudrabhishek, and chanting Shiva mantras. Most families observe all four Mondays; the general aim is happiness, prosperity, health, and the fulfilment of sincere wishes.
💍

Solah Somvar Vrat — Sixteen Mondays

A more intensive vow observed over sixteen consecutive Mondays, traditionally begun on the first Monday of Shravan. Most commonly undertaken by unmarried young women seeking a husband as devoted and steadfast as Shiva himself, and by married women praying for their husband's long life and wellbeing.
🌸

Mangala Gauri Vrat — Tuesdays

Observed on Tuesdays throughout Shravan, dedicated to Goddess Parvati (Mangala Gauri). Married women fast for a happy married life and their partner's long life; unmarried women observe it seeking a good match — mirroring the Monday vrat for Shiva but directed toward his consort.
🪐

Shani Vrat — Saturdays

Observed on Saturdays during Shravan primarily to pacify Lord Shani and gain his blessings — particularly meaningful for those navigating Sade Sati or other challenging Saturn transits.
🌗

Pradosh Vrat — Trayodashi

Observed on the thirteenth day (Trayodashi) of both the waxing and waning lunar fortnights — considered an especially powerful day for Shiva worship. Devotees keep a strict fast and perform puja specifically during Pradosh Kaal, the twilight hour.

Shravan Somvar Puja Vidhi

How to observe the sacred Monday, step by step

1
Wake Early, Bathe, Wear Clean Clothes

Begin the day before sunrise if possible. A simple bath and clean attire — light colours, ideally white or saffron — set the intention for the fast.

2
Perform Jalabhishek

Pour water — ideally Ganga water if available — over the Shivling, slowly and with intention, while chanting Om Namah Shivaya. This directly echoes the cooling waters poured over Shiva after he drank the Halahala.

3
Offer Bilva Leaves, Milk and Fruits

Bilva (bael) leaves are Shiva's most beloved offering — traditionally offered in sets of three, representing his three eyes. Milk, honey, and fresh fruit complete a simple but complete Shiva puja.

4
Chant Om Namah Shivaya and the Mahamrityunjaya Mantra

Recite Om Namah Shivaya repeatedly throughout the day. Many devotees also chant the Mahamrityunjaya Mantra, believed to protect against illness, fear, and untimely death — especially powerful during Shravan.

5
Recite the Solah Somvar Vrat Katha

If observing the 16-Monday vow, read the traditional Vrat Katha associated with it before concluding the day's puja with aarti.

6
Light a Diya in the Evening

It is considered essential to light a lamp near the Shiva image or Shivling at night, letting it burn as the fast concludes or continues into the next day depending on your family's practice.

What to Eat — and Avoid

Falahari Upvas — the fruit-based fast most commonly followed

✅
Permitted
Fruits · sabudana (sago) · sendha namak (rock salt) · milk, curd, buttermilk · nuts · water chestnut flour (singhare ka atta)
🚫
Avoided
Regular table salt · garlic and onion · grains and rice · non-vegetarian food · alcohol

Some devotees fast completely for the day, eating only after evening puja. Others eat one satvik meal, or permit fruits and light food throughout the day. Family and regional traditions vary considerably — follow whatever your household has practised, adjusted for your own health needs.

The Mantras of Shravan

Words chanted across millions of homes throughout this month

The Panchakshari Mantra

ॐ नमः शिवाय
Om Namah Shivaya
"Salutations to Shiva." The five-syllable mantra (excluding Om) at the heart of all Shaiva devotion — chanted continuously throughout Shravan, during Jalabhishek, and any time throughout the day as a simple act of remembrance.

Mahamrityunjaya Mantra

ॐ त्र्यम्बकं यजामहे सुगन्धिं पुष्टिवर्धनम्।
उर्वारुकमिव बन्धनान् मृत्योर्मुक्षीय मामृतात्॥
Om Tryambakam Yajamahe Sugandhim Pushtivardhanam
Urvarukamiva Bandhanan Mrityor Mukshiya Maamritat
"We worship the three-eyed Lord Shiva, who nourishes and nurtures all beings. As a ripe cucumber is freed from its vine, may He liberate us from death, for the sake of immortality — not from life." Widely chanted during Shravan for protection from illness, fear, and untimely death.
— Rigveda 7.59.12

Shravan for the Modern World

A month built around surrender, endurance, and cooling what burns

The God Who Held the Poison So No One Else Had To

Shiva's act during Samudra Manthan is one of the most quietly profound stories in the entire Hindu tradition. He did not destroy the poison, nor did he pass it on to someone else, nor did he let it destroy him. He held it — visibly, permanently, in his own throat — absorbing what threatened everyone, without letting it define or consume him. This is a strikingly modern image of resilience: not the absence of pain, but the capacity to hold what is difficult without either denying it or being destroyed by it. Many devotees find this the most personally meaningful part of Shravan — a month to reflect on what "poison" they themselves are holding, and how to hold it with the same steadiness Shiva showed.

For Diaspora Families — A Month, Not Just a Day

Unlike single-day festivals, Shravan offers 30 days to build a rhythm — which makes it particularly well suited to families wanting to establish a lasting practice rather than a one-off celebration.

  • Even observing just the four Mondays, rather than the full month, carries real meaning
  • A simple home Jalabhishek — water poured over even a small Shivling or image — needs no temple
  • Teach children the Samudra Manthan story — it explains why Shiva is often shown with a blue throat and a snake around his neck
  • Chanting Om Namah Shivaya together as a family, even briefly, is a meaningful daily practice for the month
  • If health permits, even a single day of Falahari fasting connects the whole family to the tradition
