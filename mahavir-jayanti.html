Who was Lord Mahavira?

The 24th Tirthankara — the ford-maker who crossed the ocean of existence

Mahavira did not create Jainism. He rediscovered and reformed it for a new age.

The word Tirthankara means "ford-maker" — one who finds and shows others the crossing point across the terrifying river of birth, death and rebirth. Jainism recognises 24 such Tirthankaras in the current cosmic cycle. Mahavira, born Vardhamana, was the 24th and last. He is not a god in the traditional sense — he is a liberated soul who reached Kevala Jnana (omniscience) through 12 years of intense meditation and self-discipline, and then spent 30 years walking across India teaching what he had realised.

The Life of Vardhamana Mahavira

A prince who left everything — and found everything

1

Royal Birth — 599 BCE

Vardhamana was born on the 13th day of the bright fortnight of Chaitra, 599 BCE, in Kundagrama near Vaishali in present-day Bihar. His father was King Siddhartha of the Ikshvaku dynasty; his mother was Queen Trishala. Both were devoted followers of the 23rd Tirthankara, Parshvanatha. Before Vardhamana's birth, Queen Trishala had fourteen auspicious dreams — each interpreted as a sign that she would give birth to a great being. Indra, king of the gods, descended to perform abhishekam on Mount Meru to honour the birth. Vardhamana grew up as a prince — educated, physically strong, and sheltered from suffering.
2

The Great Renunciation — Age 30

At age 30, two years after his parents passed away through the Jain practice of Sallekhana (voluntary fasting unto death), Vardhamana made his decision. He gave away all his possessions, removed his clothing (in the Digambara tradition), and left his palace. Like the Buddha — a near-contemporary — he walked away from everything material to find the truth of existence. But unlike the Buddha's gentler path, Mahavira's discipline was extreme: twelve years of near-complete silence, minimal food, endurance of physical hardship, and an absolute commitment to ahimsa — not harming any living being, including insects and microorganisms.
3

Kevala Jnana — Omniscience Attained

After twelve years of intense meditation, at age 42, Vardhamana attained Kevala Jnana — perfect, complete, omniscient knowledge. He became a Jina (spiritual victor) — one who has conquered the inner enemies of anger, pride, greed and deceit. He became known as Mahavira (Great Hero) for his extraordinary inner strength, and Vardhamana (one who increases) as his birth name faded. For the next 30 years he walked barefoot across India — through forests, cities, royal courts and slums — teaching what he had realised. He preached in Ardha-Magadhi, the language of the common people, making spiritual wisdom accessible to all.
4

Nirvana — 527 BCE

At age 72, Mahavira attained Nirvana (liberation from the cycle of birth and death) at Pavapuri in Bihar. According to Jain tradition, on the night of his Nirvana — which fell on Diwali — all the lamps in the heavens were lit to illuminate the darkness left by the departure of his divine knowledge. This is why Jains celebrate Diwali as the New Year — the night Mahavira's soul left the world. Pavapuri today is one of Jainism's most sacred pilgrimage sites, with a magnificent white marble temple rising from a pond of pink lotuses.

The Pancha Mahavrata

The Five Great Vows · The ethical core of Jain life

These five vows are not rules imposed from outside. They are the natural behaviour of a soul that has understood reality.

Mahavira built on four vows taught by his predecessor Parshvanatha — the 23rd Tirthankara — and added a fifth: Brahmacharya. Together they form the Pancha Mahavrata — the five great vows for Jain monks and nuns. For lay people (householders), smaller versions called Anuvratas (minor vows) apply, adapted to the practical demands of life. But the aspiration is the same: reduce harm, increase truth, live simply.

1
अहिंसा
Ahimsa — Non-Violence
The supreme and foremost vow
Non-violence in thought, word and deed toward every living being — from humans to animals to insects to microorganisms. Mahavira taught that every living being, even a blade of grass, has a soul (Jiva). Harming any soul — even in thought or speech — accumulates Karma and delays liberation. Jain monks sweep the ground before they walk, filter their water, and cover their mouths to avoid inhaling insects. This is Ahimsa taken to its logical extreme.
In the world today: Mahatma Gandhi acknowledged that his doctrine of Ahimsa as political resistance drew directly from Jain philosophy. Gandhi's Ahimsa in turn inspired Martin Luther King Jr and Nelson Mandela. The most radical idea in human history — that you can defeat violence with non-violence — was first systematised by Mahavira 2,600 years ago.
2
सत्य
Satya — Truthfulness
Truth that does not harm
To speak only what is true — and to speak it in a way that does not cause harm. Mahavira's formulation is subtle: if the truth would cause unnecessary harm, it is better to stay silent. A Jain monk cannot lie, but neither should they speak a harmful truth unnecessarily. Truth must serve both accuracy and compassion simultaneously.
In the world today: This is the most nuanced principle here — truth is not a weapon to wound with. Radical honesty without compassion is violence in disguise. Mahavira's formulation offers something modern culture struggles with: How do you tell hard truths kindly?
3
अस्तेय
Asteya — Non-Stealing
Not taking what is not freely given
Not taking anything — even something abandoned or unclaimed — without permission. This extends to intellectual property, others' time, and resources taken through exploitation. Jain monks accept only what is freely offered. They do not ask for more than they need. The principle extends even to not taking credit for others' work, or using resources beyond one's legitimate share.
In the world today: Asteya is the antidote to corporate exploitation, copyright theft, and the extractive economy. A world practicing Asteya would not over-harvest forests or exploit labour — taking only what is freely given and needed.
4
ब्रह्मचर्य
Brahmacharya — Self-Restraint
Added by Mahavira to Parshvanatha's four vows
For ascetics: complete celibacy. For householders: faithfulness to one's partner, and restraint of sensual excess. This vow was added by Mahavira specifically — his predecessor Parshvanatha had only four vows. Brahmacharya addresses the dissipation of energy through sensory indulgence. It is not about fear of pleasure, but about directing life-force toward its highest purpose: liberation.
In the world today: At its most practical: do you control your desires or do your desires control you? Brahmacharya is the discipline of choosing where to direct your attention and energy — perhaps the most relevant teaching for a world of infinite digital distraction.
5
अपरिग्रह
Aparigraha — Non-Attachment
Non-possessiveness of things and emotions
Non-attachment to material possessions, wealth, relationships and emotions. For Jain monks: owning nothing at all. For householders: limiting possessions to what is genuinely needed, and giving away the rest. Mahavira taught that attachment — not things themselves, but clinging to them — is the source of violence, suffering and the accumulation of Karma. Jain businesspeople (Jains have historically been among India's most successful traders) are known for extraordinary philanthropy — a direct expression of Aparigraha.
In the world today: Aparigraha is the foundation of sustainability. An economy built on Aparigraha would not consume beyond need. Mahavira identified 2,600 years ago that attachment to "more" is the root cause of both personal suffering and collective environmental destruction.

Anekantavada — The Doctrine of Many Truths

The most radical philosophical contribution of Jainism to the world

The Most Important Idea You've Never Heard Of
No single perspective contains the complete truth.
Mahavira taught Anekantavada — literally "many-sidedness" or "non-absolutism." Reality is complex and multi-faceted. Because every human being perceives reality from a limited perspective (their body, their senses, their conditioning, their position in space and time), no single viewpoint can grasp the whole truth. Therefore — certainty about having the complete truth is itself a form of ignorance.

The corollary is Syadvada — "maybe" logic. Every statement should be prefaced with "in some respect" — acknowledging that from another valid perspective, the opposite might also be true. This is not relativism (the idea that all views are equally valid). It is the recognition that truth is larger than any one person's grasp of it.
"All breathing, existing, living, sentient creatures should not be slain, nor treated with violence, nor abused, nor tormented, nor driven away. This is the pure, eternal, unchangeable law."
— Lord Mahavira · Acaranga Sutra

Why Anekantavada matters in 2026

Every major conflict in the world today — political, religious, cultural — is driven by people who are certain their view is the only correct one. Anekantavada is the philosophical antidote to this. It does not ask you to abandon your view. It asks you to hold it with appropriate humility, knowing that the person opposing you may be seeing a real aspect of truth that you cannot see from where you stand. Gandhi used Anekantavada as the basis for dialogue with his opponents. Modern interfaith dialogue is built on the same principle. It is perhaps Jainism's greatest gift to the world.

Jainism and the Indic Traditions

Shared roots · Distinct paths · One civilisational heritage

The Sanatana Series covers Jainism, Buddhism and Sikhism alongside Hinduism because these are not separate civilisations. They share the same Indic philosophical soil — the same vocabulary of karma, dharma, moksha, ahimsa and the cycle of rebirth. They arose in the same geographic, cultural and spiritual context, often in dialogue and debate with each other. Presenting them together is not about claiming one as superior to another — it is recognising the extraordinary depth of the Indic intellectual and spiritual tradition as a whole.

🔄
Shared Concepts with Hinduism

Karma, Moksha (liberation from rebirth), Dharma (right conduct), Ahimsa, the cycle of Samsara (birth and death), meditation practice, the Sanskrit-derived sacred vocabulary — all shared. Mahavira's parents were devotees of the previous Tirthankara, showing that Jainism was already embedded in the same cultural and spiritual world as the Vedic tradition. The difference: Jainism does not accept the authority of the Vedas, rejects ritual sacrifice, and holds that liberation comes from self-effort alone — not divine grace.

☸️
Relationship with Buddhism

Mahavira and the Buddha were near-contemporaries — both born in Bihar within decades of each other, both renounced royal comfort, both rejected Vedic authority and caste hierarchy, both taught in the common language rather than Sanskrit, both emphasised personal effort over divine intervention, both attracted large followings. The Buddhist scriptures frequently mention the "Niganthas" (Jains) and record debates between Buddhist and Jain monks. The two traditions shaped each other through centuries of respectful philosophical competition.

🪯
The Broader Dharmic World

Sikhism, which will be covered in a future Sanatana Series guide, drew from both the Hindu Bhakti movement and Sufi Islam, while sharing the same vocabulary of karma, seva (selfless service) and the search for liberation. Together, Hinduism, Jainism, Buddhism and Sikhism form the great Indic dharmic tradition — diverse in form and practice, unified in their deepest concerns: How should we live? What is the nature of the self? How do we find freedom from suffering?

Mahavir Jayanti — How It Is Celebrated

31 March 2026 · Temples · Processions · Charity

🛕

Abhisheka — The Sacred Bath

The central ritual of Mahavir Jayanti: a ceremonial bathing of Mahavira's idol with water, milk, and fragrant oils — symbolising purity. In Jain temples across India, the Abhisheka ceremony begins before dawn. At sites like Shravanabelagola in Karnataka (home to the colossal 18-metre statue of Gomateshwara) and Palitana in Gujarat (the only mountain in the world with over 800 temples), the day draws hundreds of thousands of devotees.
🎭

Rath Yatra — The Procession

Beautifully decorated chariots carry an idol of Mahavira through the streets of Jain communities. Monks and nuns walk in procession — barefoot, in white robes (or unclothed, for Digambara monks). The air fills with Jain hymns praising the Tirthankaras. In cities like Surat, Jaipur, Kolkata and Mumbai with large Jain populations, these processions draw massive community participation.
🍱

Charity and Feeding — Annadana

Jains are known for extraordinary philanthropy — a direct expression of Aparigraha (non-possessiveness). On Mahavir Jayanti, large-scale feeding of the poor (Annadana), donation to hospitals and educational institutions, and support for animal welfare organisations — shelters for cows and birds especially — are organised across India. The Jain community's charitable network is one of the most extensive of any religious group in India.
📖

Discourses and Scripture Reading

Jain scholars and monks deliver discourses on Mahavira's teachings at temples and community halls. The Kalpasutra — a Jain text containing biographies of the Tirthankaras, particularly Mahavira — is read aloud. In the Shvetambara tradition, elaborately illustrated manuscripts of the Kalpasutra are displayed. Communities gather for Swadhyaya (self-study and reflection on Jain texts).

Jainism's Legacy in the World

The oldest continuously practised religion in India · Global impact

Ahimsa Changed the World

The most direct line from Mahavira to the modern world runs through Gandhi. Gandhi's satyagraha — truth-force — was explicitly built on Jain Ahimsa. Gandhi attended Jain temples, befriended Jain monks, and credited Jain philosophy as central to his political method. Gandhi's Ahimsa in turn directly inspired Martin Luther King Jr's Civil Rights Movement, Nelson Mandela's resistance in South Africa, and peace movements worldwide. Mahavira's teaching — that violence always creates more violence — is the foundational insight of non-violent resistance as a political strategy.

Jains and Indian Commerce

The Jain community — roughly 6 million in India — has been disproportionately influential in Indian commerce, finance and industry for two thousand years. The Jain merchant communities of Gujarat, Rajasthan and Maharashtra built trading networks spanning Asia, Africa and Europe. Their commercial ethics — based on Satya (truthfulness in transactions) and Aparigraha (not accumulating beyond need) — established standards of trust that made Jain merchants globally respected. Many of India's most prominent business families are Jain.

Environmental Ethics

Jainism is arguably the world's oldest environmentalist religion. Its insistence on the soulfulness of all living beings — trees, rivers, insects — means that harming the natural world is not merely inconvenient but spiritually harmful. The concept of Aparigraha (taking only what you need) is the foundation of sustainable economics. As the world grapples with climate change and resource depletion, Jain environmental ethics offer a 2,600-year-old philosophical framework for living within natural limits.
