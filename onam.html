🕊️ The Festival That Belongs to Everyone

Onam is genuinely unique among major Indian festivals: it is celebrated across every community in Kerala — Hindu, Christian and Muslim alike — as a shared cultural and harvest celebration that transcends religious lines. Kerala's government treats it as the state's official festival, with public holidays across the entire ten-day period. This is one of the rare celebrations in India that unifies people regardless of faith — everyone welcomes the same king home.

What is Onam?

A harvest festival, a homecoming, and Kerala's proudest ten days of the year

Onam marks the Malayalam month of Chingam — Kerala's New Year season and the peak of the harvest — but at its heart, it is a story about welcoming someone home.

According to legend, King Mahabali was once the ruler of Kerala — an asura (demon) king by lineage, but a monarch whose reign is remembered as a golden age of prosperity, equality and justice, where no one lied, no one stole, and everyone lived in harmony regardless of caste or status. His popularity grew so great that even the gods felt threatened by it. This is the story of what happened next — and why, every year, Kerala still prepares for his one-day return.

King Mahabali and the Vamana Avatar

The story behind why Kerala welcomes a king back every single year

1

The King Too Beloved for His Own Good

Mahabali, grandson of the great devotee Prahlad, ruled with such fairness and generosity that his kingdom became a genuine utopia — abundance for all, no crime, no inequality, no suffering. His subjects loved him completely. But this very perfection troubled the Devas, who worried that a demon king's rising glory and growing power might eventually threaten the cosmic balance between gods and asuras.
2

The Dwarf Who Asked for Three Steps

Lord Vishnu descended in his fifth avatar — Vamana, a humble dwarf Brahmin — and approached Mahabali during a great sacrificial ceremony where the king was granting wishes to all who asked. Vamana requested something modest-sounding: as much land as he could cover in three paces. Mahabali, true to his generous nature and against the warning of his guru Shukracharya (who recognised Vishnu in disguise), happily agreed.
3

Two Steps — And Nothing Left But a Head

The moment Mahabali agreed, Vamana grew to cosmic proportions. With his first step he covered the entire earth; with his second, the whole of the heavens. There was nowhere left for a third step. True to his word even in complete defeat, Mahabali — having nothing else left to offer — bowed his head and asked Vishnu to place his final step upon it. Moved by this ultimate act of humility and honesty, Vishnu pressed Mahabali down into the netherworld (Patala) with his foot — but did not destroy him.
4

One Wish Granted — The Wish to Come Home

Impressed by Mahabali's unwavering devotion, honesty, and love for his people even as he lost his kingdom and freedom, Vishnu granted him a single boon: he could return to Kerala once every year to see his beloved subjects and witness that they still lived in the prosperity and harmony he had built. Onam is that homecoming — the ten days when Kerala prepares its finest welcome for a king who lost everything except the love of his people, and the honesty to keep his word even when it cost him his throne.

The Traditions of Onam

What makes this festival so visually and culturally distinct

🌸

Pookalam — The Flower Carpet

Every morning of the ten days, women of the household lay a fresh, colourful floral design at the entrance of the home, growing larger and more intricate each day. The Pookalam welcomes Mahabali's spirit and grows in complexity until Thiruvonam, when it reaches its most elaborate form using dozens of harvest-season blooms native to Kerala.
🍽️

Onam Sadya — The Grand Feast

A vegetarian feast of 26 or more dishes, served traditionally on a fresh banana leaf and eaten with the hands. Guests, family, and neighbours are welcomed generously — echoing Mahabali's own open-hearted rule, where hospitality was never withheld from anyone.
🚣

Vallamkali — Snake Boat Races

Long, ornately decorated snake boats (Chundan Vallam), each rowed by upwards of 100 people, race along Kerala's backwaters to the rhythm of traditional Vanchipattu boat songs. The races are among the most spectacular and widely attended public events of the entire festival.
🐅

Pulikali — The Tiger Dance

Performers paint their bodies as tigers and hunters and dance through the streets to energetic drumbeats — a folk-art tradition especially associated with Thrissur, drawing enormous crowds and considered one of Onam's most theatrical public spectacles.

How to Celebrate Onam

Simple, joyful, and needs no temple at all

1
Lay a Pookalam at Your Doorstep

Even a small flower arrangement grown day by day carries the spirit of the tradition — it doesn't need to be elaborate to be meaningful.

2
Wear New Clothes — Ideally Kasavu

Onakkodi — new clothing — is traditionally worn on Thiruvonam. The classic Kerala kasavu (cream and gold bordered saree or mundu) is especially treasured for the day.

3
Prepare or Share an Onam Sadya

A full 26-dish feast is a wonderful goal, but even a handful of Sadya staples — rice, sambar, avial, payasam — served on a banana leaf carries the meal's spirit fully.

4
Welcome Guests Generously

In the spirit of Mahabali's open kingdom, invite neighbours, friends, and especially anyone who might otherwise spend the day alone or far from family.

5
Watch or Join in the Festivities

If a Vallamkali boat race or Pulikali performance is happening nearby, it's worth experiencing — these are some of the most visually spectacular traditions in the entire Hindu festival calendar.

Onam for the Modern World

A harvest festival built entirely around welcome, honesty, and shared abundance

A King Remembered for Losing Well

Mahabali is one of the rare figures in any mythology celebrated not for a victory, but for how gracefully and honestly he accepted defeat. He kept his word even when it cost him everything. He remained beloved by his people even after losing his throne. Onam is, at its core, a festival about what it means to rule — and to lose — with dignity, generosity and truth intact.

For Diaspora Families — Kerala's Global Homecoming

Onam travels remarkably well. Large Malayali communities in the Gulf states, the United States, and the United Kingdom organise Onam melas — community feasts, cultural performances, and games — keeping the festival firmly alive far from Kerala's shores.

  • Tell children the Vamana story — a rare tale where the "loser" is the one everyone still loves
  • Even a simple Pookalam at the door is a meaningful, visual way to mark the day
  • Don't waste the Sadya — serving modestly and finishing what's on the leaf is part of the tradition's spirit
  • Invite someone who might otherwise be alone — this is the single most important part of Onam's ethic
  • Remember Onam belongs to everyone in Kerala regardless of religion — a genuinely rare unifying celebration worth honouring as such
