A wedding in the courtyard

On the wedding evening, the same Tulsi that a family waters through the year may stand under a little canopy of sugarcane. A chunri rests against the planter, flowers arrive one handful at a time, and a lamp is set beside the plant. The household has prepared for a wedding. Tulsi Devi is honoured through the living plant, and Vishnu is received as the groom in the form of a Shaligram or an image of Vishnu or Krishna.

Vivah means marriage. A mandap is the canopy under which the rite takes place. A Tulsi Vrindavan is the planter or pedestal in which the plant is tended and worshipped. A wedding cloth, prayers and the sharing of prasad can turn the everyday space around it into a place for the whole family to gather. The wedding gives ceremonial form to the affection for Tulsi already expressed in daily Vaishnava worship. ISKCON Navi Mumbai · Mumbai region heritage study

The sacred stories of Tulsi

How did a plant tended in the courtyard come to stand beside Vishnu at a wedding? Two sacred stories lead to that familiar sight. One begins with a queen’s disturbing dream, the other with a promise made on a battlefield.

Vrinda and Jalandhara — A story of devotion and betrayal

A telling from the Shiva Purana

Toward dawn, Vrinda dreamed that the sea rose over her city and swept her under with it. She woke to a rising sun that seemed to fade before her eyes. Her husband, Jalandhara, had gone to fight Shiva. She could get no rest in the palace, so she went with two friends to the city garden. Even there she could find no peace, and she wandered on into the forest.

Jalandhara was king of the asuras and a son of the ocean. When he learned that the gods had churned the sea and taken its treasures, he sent a messenger to Indra asking for them back. Indra defended what he had done. Jalandhara marched against the gods, and the war that followed brought him face to face with Shiva. Far from the battlefield, Vrinda had only her dreams to go on.

In the forest, two fearsome figures came after her. Vrinda took refuge with an ascetic, who drove them away with a shout. Once she was safe, she asked him the question that had brought her no peace since dawn. How had her husband fared? The ascetic gave her no answer. At a sign from him, two monkeys went away and returned with Jalandhara’s remains. Vrinda fainted at the sight. When she came to, she mourned the man she had loved and remembered how she had warned him about taking on Shiva. Then she asked the ascetic to bring him back.

Jalandhara seemed to rise again. For a time Vrinda’s joy was such that the grief she had just felt seemed like a dream. Then she discovered the deception. The ascetic had been Vishnu, and the man who had embraced her and slept with her had also been Vishnu, wearing her husband’s form. The frightening dreams had been part of his illusion. Vrinda rebuked him. She cursed him to endure the loss of his own wife and to seek help among monkeys in the forest. In her words, listeners can hear an echo of Rama’s separation from Sita.

Vrinda entered the fire despite Vishnu’s attempt to stop her. He stayed by her pyre, grieving. In time the gods brought seeds given by the goddesses and sowed them where the fire had burned. Holy basil, amla and malati grew there. Tulsi and amla were especially dear to Vishnu, and his sorrow eased. In this telling, the plant beloved of Vishnu first grows at the place where he mourns Vrinda. When a family adorns Tulsi for the wedding, this telling gives them a reason to remember Vrinda.

Tulasi and Shankhachuda — The story behind Tulsi and Shaligram

A telling from the Devi Bhagavata Purana

Before an old Brahmin would say what he wanted, he asked the king to promise to give it. Shankhachuda agreed. The man asked for the kavacha at his neck, the sacred protection he wore into battle. The king kept his word and gave it to him. The Brahmin was Vishnu in disguise.

Shankhachuda was a king of the danavas and a devotee of Krishna. He had won Tulasi’s hand after long austerities. Now he was at war with the gods, and Shiva had come to fight him. Before taking up his bow, Shankhachuda stepped down from his chariot and bowed to Shiva. The battle that followed reached a standstill. A boon protected Shankhachuda as long as he wore the kavacha and his wife’s fidelity remained inviolate. Vishnu had taken the first protection from him. He then took the king’s form and went to Tulasi’s home.

Drums sounded at the door and voices called out in victory. Tulasi looked from her window and saw the man she believed to be her husband home from war. She gave gifts in celebration, washed his feet and wept with joy. She made him sit and asked how the fighting had ended. He told her that Brahma had brought about peace. She had received him with the affection she felt for her husband. Vishnu let her believe that was who he was when he slept with her.

On the battlefield, Shiva raised his trident. Shankhachuda put down his weapons and turned his mind to Krishna. The trident struck him. The king who had given away his protection died as a devotee, meditating on the deity he loved.

Tulasi sensed that the man with her was different and demanded to know who he was. Vishnu revealed himself. Tulasi grieved for Shankhachuda and challenged Vishnu for deceiving her and bringing about the death of a man who was himself a devotee. She called his heart hard as stone and pronounced her curse. Vishnu would become stone himself.

Vishnu accepted the curse. Tulasi took a divine form and went with him, and the story says that her earthly body became the Gandaki River and her hair became the Tulsi plant. Vishnu took a stone form by the Gandaki, where the story places the sacred Shaligrams. In worship, the leaf is offered to the stone. At Tulsi Vivah, the plant and Shaligram come together as bride and groom. The story keeps Tulasi’s protest in memory alongside their union.

Why do the traditions tell different stories?

Different Puranas remember Tulsi through different sacred stories. In the Shiva Purana, Vrinda curses Vishnu to be parted from his wife, and Tulsi grows where Vrinda’s pyre stood. The Padma Purana remembers Vrinda’s final meditation by a lake and links Tulsi’s seed to Gauri in a separate Kartik passage. In the Devi Bhagavata Purana, it is Shankhachuda’s wife Tulasi who speaks the stone curse, and her story leads to the Gandaki.

Some popular retellings give the stone curse to Vrinda and tell its outcome as part of the Jalandhara story. Each account has its own characters and ending, and each offers a way to understand why Tulsi is dear to Vishnu.

A leaf for Vishnu

In many Vaishnava traditions, Tulsi leaves accompany food or flowers offered to Vishnu and Krishna. A leaf grown and cared for at home can be part of what the family brings to worship. The Devi Bhagavata Purana praises offering Tulsi in Kartik and describes the reverence due to the plant. Its following chapter also marks Kartik Purnima as a celebration of Tulasi’s birth. Devi Bhagavata Purana 9.24 · 9.25

A Shaligram is revered as a sacred form of Vishnu. Many are dark ammonite fossils from Nepal’s Kali Gandaki valley, sometimes with a visible spiral or other natural markings. A household may bring its Shaligram into the wedding as the groom, beside the rooted plant that embodies Tulsi Devi. Some worshippers use a murti, a sacred image, instead. When Vishnu awakens

Chaturmas means four months. In one widely followed Vaishnava reckoning, the period begins with Devshayani Ekadashi in Ashadha, when Vishnu is understood to enter his seasonal rest. It closes around Dev Uthani or Prabodhini Ekadashi in Kartik. The interval is associated with monsoon-season vows and worship. Communities differ in how they mark its start and conclusion. Drik Panchang, Chaturmas

Families that pause auspicious marriages during Chaturmas often regard Vishnu’s awakening and Tulsi’s wedding as the return of wedding season. The first wedding in this sense belongs to the courtyard shrine. Families choose dates for human weddings with their own calendar and customs. Drik Panchang, Chaturmasya tradition

From the first flowers to aarti

A family may celebrate with a few lamps and prayers, or invite relatives and neighbours for a fuller wedding. This sequence describes elements a visitor might see. The order, songs and roles vary. ISKCON Navi Mumbai · Mumbai region heritage study

  1. Clear the courtyard. Sweep around the plant and clean or decorate the Tulsi Vrindavan. Some households lay a rangoli by the planter.
  2. Set up the mandap. A small canopy, sometimes supported by sugarcane stalks, marks the place for the wedding. Garlands dress the frame.
  3. Dress Tulsi. A chunri, flowers or bridal adornments may be arranged around the planter, leaving the living leaves free.
  4. Welcome Vishnu. Place the Shaligram on a seat or small dish beside the plant, or bring the family’s image of Vishnu or Krishna.
  5. Bring the offerings. Families may set out flowers, lamps, seasonal fruit and sweets. Each household has its own customary prasad.
  6. Make the wedding prayers. Songs, garlands, a wedding cloth or circumambulation may form part of the rite, depending on the tradition.
  7. Close with aarti. The lamp is offered in worship, and prasad is shared with those who have gathered.

What goes on the thali?

Vrindavan and Shaligram
The planter tends the living Tulsi. A smaller dish or seat beside it can hold the sacred stone for worship.
Sugarcane and marigolds
Sugarcane may provide the uprights of a simple mandap. Garlands bring the colour of a wedding to the canopy and planter.
Chunri, flowers and thread
The red cloth dresses the bride’s place. Flowers and ceremonial thread are used according to the household’s custom.
Diya, brass thali and prasad
A small clay or brass lamp provides the flame for worship. A thali gathers the materials, while seasonal fruit and sweets can be offered and shared.

Different ways to keep the wedding

North India. Some Braj communities celebrate the wedding in Krishna temples, bringing in wedding garlands, songs and a procession. Households may instead gather around their own planter. ISKCON Raichur

Maharashtra and Goa. A heritage study of Deulwadi, Uran, describes Tulsi planters decorated with sugarcane, mango leaves and flowers on Dwadashi. In some Marathi ceremonies the antarpat, a cloth held between the two sacred forms, is lowered after wedding verses. A Goa temple’s published calendar sets its Tulsi wedding on Kartik Shukla Dwadashi. These are local examples. Deulwadi heritage study · ISKCON Raichur · Goa Official Gazette

Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. Telugu communities call the ceremony Tulasi Kalyanam, often on Ksheerabdhi Dwadashi. In some households an amla branch accompanies Tulsi as a form of Vishnu. In other Vaishnava communities, the temple’s Kartik calendar may place the wedding at the full moon. ISKCON Raichur

Diaspora. A garden or balcony planter can be as familiar a centre for household worship as a courtyard. Temples may set a public programme to suit their community. For example, Berlin Temple in New Jersey announced a Sunday gathering in 2025 for the wedding of Tulsi and Shaligram. For 2026, check your own temple’s announcement and local panchang separately. India Temple Association, New Jersey

Tulsi, the plant tended through the year

Holy basil, Ocimum tenuiflorum L., is an aromatic member of the mint family. Kew lists it as native across tropical and subtropical Asia into the western Pacific. A family may water its plant and light a lamp near it as part of ordinary worship. On Tulsi Vivah, a living thing already looked after each day becomes the centre of a wedding. Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew · Devi Bhagavata Purana 9.25

Tulsi also appears in traditional Indian materia medica. A record of traditional use and clinical evidence answer different questions. This guide describes the plant’s place in household and devotional life. Siddha Pharmacopoeia of India

What the courtyard holds

After the guests leave, the plant remains. Someone will water it again, someone will remember the song their grandmother sang, and someone will carry the last sweets to a neighbour. The festival gives an evening to commitments that take a year to keep. For readers today, that can be a way to think about devotion, gratitude and the care owed to the living things and people that sustain a household.
